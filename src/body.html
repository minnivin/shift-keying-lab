What do these terms mean?
Carrier: the steady wave a radio changes to carry bits. Bandwidth: frequency space used (kHz). Less is better. BER: fraction of received bits that are wrong. Lower is better. Eb/N0: signal vs noise per bit (dB). Higher is cleaner.

Equations with your numbers, and assumptions
  • Carrier amplitude is 1 V into 1 Ω for every scheme. Eb is the energy of one bit. ASK is compared at the same peak amplitude as the others, so on average on-off ASK uses half the energy per bit. That is why it sits about 3 dB behind FSK, the same gap the Lecture 7 graph shows. At equal average energy it would match FSK. PSK, FSK and 16-QAM use the same average power.
  • Noise is white Gaussian (AWGN) and the receiver is coherent: it knows the carrier frequency and phase exactly.
  • FSK tone spacing is a multiple of Rb/2, so the two tones are orthogonal. PSK here is binary PSK: two phases, 0° and 180°. 16-QAM is Gray coded: 2 bits set the I level and 2 bits set the Q level.
  • Bits are rectangular pulses, so the spectrum is sinc-shaped. BWNOM is the main lobe, first null to first null. It holds about 90 % of the power for PSK and 16-QAM, and more for ASK and FSK. The spikes (carrier, FSK tones) are lines, so their height on the plot depends on how finely the spectrum is calculated.
  • The BER formulas and the definition of Eb/N0 are standard communications theory, not from the course slides, which only show BER graphs. The curves match the Lecture 7 graph values (slide 9).
  • The noise tick box adds noise to the waveforms, puts a white-noise floor (N0) on the spectrum, and scatters the I-Q constellation. The I-Q axes are normalised so that √Eb = 1 (16-QAM: average symbol energy 1), and the receiver picks the nearest ideal symbol.
  • "BER simulated" sends 200 000 random bits through the noisy channel and counts the errors: BER = bit errors / total bits (Lecture 6).
